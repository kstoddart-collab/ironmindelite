Identity Before Performance

Week 2 · Foundation

Facilitator guide · Candidate exercises · Coaching demo · Practice lab · Fieldwork · Evaluator rubric

Facilitator guide

Core IronMind principle. Performance can matter deeply while representing only one part of a person’s identity. Help the athlete name what the role means to them and recognize qualities they can carry into other roles.

By the end of the session, candidates can distinguish a result, a role and a personal quality, reflect the meaning of a threatened role, and help an athlete choose or defer a next step.

Use the following 90-minute plan for a live cohort. Bring the case, exercise prompts, a timer and a copy of the rubric for each observer.

  1. Opening · 10 minutes. Ask candidates to recall Week 1’s listening practice. Open with “What can someone lose when a season ends besides the chance to compete?” Invite people to share only what they choose.
  2. Learn It · 15 minutes. Teach three distinctions with a single example. “I missed qualifying” is an outcome. “I’m the team’s lead runner” is a role. “I show up for teammates” is a quality supported by behavior. Ask what the role gave the athlete and where that quality already shows up. Make room for disappointment before exploring the second question.
  3. Live It · 15 minutes. Give candidates eight minutes to work through their identity exercise. Use the remaining time for pairs to share one quality and the behavior that supports it.
  4. Coaching demo · 15 minutes. Read the script with a volunteer. Ask observers to mark the first accurate reflection, the question that uncovers evidence outside sport and the moment the athlete chooses.
  5. Practice lab · 30 minutes. Run three rotations of the triad exercise below.
  6. Debrief and fieldwork · 5 minutes. Ask each candidate to name one behavior to repeat in the field and one habit to watch for.

Discussion prompt. How would you respond if the athlete said, “I know people care about me, but I still want my spot back”? Ask candidates to reflect both the ambition and the uncertainty before asking another question.

Candidate exercises

Learn It · Read the athlete’s words

“Everyone keeps telling me I should be proud, but I feel like if I stop performing at this level, I won’t know what I have left.”

  1. Write one sentence about what the athlete actually said.
  2. Write two plausible meanings, such as losing belonging or losing a way to contribute. Turn one interpretation into a reflection the athlete can correct.
  3. Write one open question that would help you learn what the role means to this athlete.

Live It · Map your own identity

Pick a role or result that once mattered to you. In your workbook, write what happened, what it meant to you, and one quality you brought to the role. Give a specific example of using that quality in another setting. Finish with “When a candidate or athlete hesitates, my first impulse is…” and name one response you want to practice.

Coach It · Return the decision

Write a response for when an athlete offers two options and says, “I don’t know.” Include a question that helps them weigh the options and a separate invitation to choose, defer or suggest another option. If they choose a step, ask what follow-up would help them.

Coaching demo

Athlete. “Everyone keeps telling me I should be proud, but I feel like if I stop performing at this level, I won’t know what I have left.”

Coach. “What feels most at risk if your role changes?”

Athlete. “Being somebody people count on. At school, I’m the runner.”

Coach. “Running is a place where you know you matter. Losing that role feels bigger than losing a season. Is that close?”

Athlete. “Yeah. My sister says I’m the person she calls when she needs help, but that just feels like normal stuff.”

Coach. “What does your sister count on you for?”

Athlete. “I show up. I listen. The younger runners ask me for help sometimes, too. I could talk to my sister or help with warmups. I don’t know which would help.”

Coach. “Take a moment. What would you want from each of those options?”

Athlete. “Helping with warmups would keep me involved. Talking to my sister would help me say what I’ve been worried about.”

Coach. “What feels useful right now? It’s okay to give it more thought.”

Athlete. “I want to talk to my sister this weekend.”

Coach. “How, if at all, would you like to follow up?”

Athlete. “Ask me how it went next time we meet.”

Evaluator debrief. “Why did you choose that approach?” The coach can point to the athlete’s own words about being counted on, the example involving their sister, and the athlete’s stated choice and follow-up.

Practice lab

Work in triads as coach, athlete and observer. Rotate every 10 minutes. Use six minutes for coaching, two for feedback and two to replay the closing exchange.

  • Athlete. Start with the case opening. If asked what feels at risk, say that being the runner is how people know you. If the coach explores other settings, bring up your sister or the younger runners. Near the close, offer two options and hesitate. Choose, defer or propose another option based on the conversation.
  • Coach. Reflect the meaning of the role and check your interpretation. Ask for a concrete example of a quality in action. Pause when the athlete hesitates. Let them decide what happens next.
  • Observer. Write down one exact reflection and the line where the athlete made or deferred a choice. Ask “Why did you choose that approach?” Give one behavior to keep and one to replay. Score the replay separately so the candidate can see the change.

If the athlete says “What would you do?”, practice acknowledging the invitation and first asking what they want each option to accomplish.

Fieldwork

Before Week 3, hold one brief practice conversation with a consenting peer or an approved participant working through an ordinary role change. Use your program’s boundaries for field practice. If a need arises outside your coaching role, follow the program’s support process.

Add an entry to your portfolio with the general topic, the skill practiced, what you noticed, what worked, what was difficult, what you would change, and what you learned about yourself. Include an anonymized example of your reflection and record who chose the next step and shaped the follow-up. Omit identifying details.

Reflection. Where were you most tempted to interpret, reassure or solve for the person? What will you try in your next conversation?

Evaluator rubric

Rate each criterion using observable evidence. Use Significant concern for a serious or repeated problem such as overriding expressed choices after correction or mishandling private information. Use Evidence missing when the behavior was unavailable to observe. Record the evidence and the action needed before reassessing.

Identity before performance
Meets standard when the candidate honors what the role means and helps the athlete name a quality with evidence beyond performance. Needs development when they dismiss the role or supply a quality without checking it.
Listening and curiosity
Meets standard when the candidate checks a specific reflection and follows the athlete’s language with an open question. Needs development when reassurance or leading questions cut off exploration.
Client ownership and close
Meets standard when the candidate gives the athlete room to weigh options, choose or defer, and shape any follow-up. Needs development when the candidate selects the action, deadline or follow-up for them.
Judgment and boundaries
Meets standard when the candidate explains a decision using the athlete’s cues, stays within the coaching role, and protects privacy in their notes. Needs development when they give a generic rationale or need prompting to recognize where their role ends.

Week 2 check-off. Record evidence for each criterion from the lab, debrief or fieldwork as appropriate. Check off Week 2 when all criteria meet the standard and the identity exercise and fieldwork entry are in the portfolio. For a specific gap, assign a targeted replay with a fresh prompt and document what the candidate needs to demonstrate.
