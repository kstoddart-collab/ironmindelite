Pressure, Awareness, and the Performance Gap

Week 3

Facilitator guide · Candidate exercises · Coaching demo · Practice lab · Fieldwork · Evaluator rubric

Facilitator guide

Core IronMind principle. Awareness creates room for a choice under pressure. For this lesson, the performance gap is the distance between the response an athlete intends and what they actually do in a specific moment.

By the end of the session, candidates can describe that gap using observable behavior, help an athlete identify an early pressure cue, and coach the athlete to choose and rehearse a controllable response.

  1. Opening · 10 minutes. Revisit Week 2. Ask what happens to a runner’s attention when a race feels like a test of who they are. Invite candidates to describe a moment they are comfortable sharing.
  2. Learn It · 15 minutes. Put five columns on the board: situation, early cue, actual response, intended response, and next action. Use the runner’s case. Hearing a rival surge is the situation, tight hands are the cue, and speeding up immediately is the actual response. Holding the planned pace until the next marker is the intended response. Ask which details a coach could observe and which only the athlete could report.
  3. Live It · 15 minutes. Give candidates eight minutes to map a moment when pressure changed their own behavior. Pair them up to check that each proposed action can be performed at the moment the cue appears.
  4. Coaching demo · 15 minutes. Read the script with a volunteer. Ask the group to identify the point when the athlete first spots a cue and the point when they choose what to try.
  5. Practice lab · 30 minutes. Run three ten-minute rotations using the instructions below.
  6. Debrief and fieldwork · 5 minutes. Have each candidate name one question they will use and one way they will tell whether the chosen response was attempted.

Discussion. An athlete follows their plan and still loses. What evidence would you use to review the response? How would you talk about the result while keeping the athlete’s identity separate from it?

Candidate exercises

Learn It · Map the gap

A runner intends to hold their opening pace until the first marker. When a rival passes, the runner grips their hands, watches the rival and surges early. Write the situation, earliest reported cue, actual response and intended response. List one detail you would confirm before suggesting a rehearsal.

Live It · Catch your own cue

Choose a recent pressure moment. Write what you intended to do and what you did. Recall the first cue you noticed in your body, attention or self-talk. Choose one small action you can control and complete the sentence “When I notice ___, I will ___.” Rehearse twice and revise the action if it feels too complicated to use in the moment.

Coach It · Turn awareness into action

Write one question to locate the earliest cue and one that helps the athlete choose a response. Then write how you would invite a brief rehearsal and ask what the athlete wants to track. Pick an observation the athlete can make regardless of the result.

Coaching demo

The Week 2 runner is back and wants to understand what happens when someone passes early in a race.

Athlete. “I tell myself I’ll run my plan, but as soon as somebody goes by, I chase. It feels like I’m already losing my spot.”

Coach. “Walk me through the last time. What did you plan to do, and what happened?”

Athlete. “Hold pace until the first marker. When I heard footsteps, my hands clenched. I watched them go by and accelerated before the marker.”

Coach. “Being passed felt urgent. Could you notice your hands before your pace changed?”

Athlete. “Yes, right when I heard the footsteps.”

Coach. “What could you do at that point that would help you stay with your plan?”

Athlete. “Unclench my hands and look at the marker.”

Coach. “Want to try it now? Picture the footsteps, notice your hands, and show me what you’d do.”

Athlete. “I open my hands, look ahead and hold pace to the marker. That feels like something I can remember.”

Coach. “When would you like to test it, and how will you know whether you tried it?”

Athlete. “At practice on Thursday. After the rep I’ll note whether I caught the cue and held pace to the marker.”

Evaluator debrief. “Why did you choose that approach?” The coach explains how the athlete’s earliest cue created an opportunity to act and how the athlete’s practice check measures their response.

Practice lab

Work in triads as coach, athlete and observer. Rotate every ten minutes. Coach for six minutes, give feedback for two, then replay one exchange for two.

  • Athlete. Say you sped up when a rival passed. Reveal what happened just before the surge when the coach asks. If you hear a vague instruction like “stay calm,” ask what you could actually do in the moment.
  • Coach. Reconstruct one instance. Confirm the intended and actual responses, ask about the earliest cue, invite an athlete-chosen action, rehearse it and agree on how the athlete wants to check it in practice.
  • Observer. Capture the question that surfaced the cue, the athlete’s own words for the action and the agreed practice check. Give feedback on one behavior and score the replay separately.

For a fresh replay, use a player who rushes their next free throw after a miss. Keep the sequence and let the athlete decide what action fits.

Fieldwork

Before Week 4, hold one practice conversation with a consenting peer or approved participant about an ordinary performance pressure moment. Map the gap together, rehearse one response, and let the participant choose when to test it and whether to follow up.

In your portfolio, record the general topic, reported cue, intended and actual responses, chosen action, rehearsal, and what you would change. Omit identifying details. If the participant reports back, capture whether they noticed the cue and attempted the action. Record an untested response as planned.

Reflection. What did the participant notice that you would have missed? How did you respond if they wanted to choose an action different from the one you had in mind?

Evaluator rubric

Cite an observed behavior or submitted item for each rating. Use Significant concern for serious or repeated issues such as disregarding the participant’s choice after feedback or mishandling private information. Use Evidence missing when you cannot assess a criterion from available evidence.

Defines the performance gap
Meets standard when the candidate confirms the intended and actual behaviors in a specific situation. Needs development when the gap stays general or becomes a label about the athlete.
Builds awareness
Meets standard when the candidate checks an early cue in the athlete’s own words and locates it before the response. Needs development when they assume the cue or identify only what happened afterward.
Rehearses a controllable response
Meets standard when the athlete chooses and tries a feasible response tied to the cue. Needs development when the coach prescribes the response or the action stays too vague to rehearse.
Closes with ownership and sound judgment
Meets standard when the athlete shapes the practice check, the candidate explains their choices from the conversation, and notes protect privacy. Needs development when the coach sets follow-up unilaterally or uses the competitive result as the sole measure.

Week 3 check-off. Record evidence from the lab, debrief and portfolio. Mark complete when all four criteria meet the standard and the candidate submits the pressure map and fieldwork entry. For a specific gap, use a fresh scenario and reassess the behavior in question.
