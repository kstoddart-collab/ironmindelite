Identity, confidence and resilience

Five field coaching sessions

  1. After a setback, I reflected the person’s frustration. I offered a suggestion before asking what they wanted from the conversation.
  2. Before a tryout, I asked what they could control. They named their warmup routine. Next time, I’ll ask one question at a time and wait.
  3. After losing a starting role, they named helping newer players as a contribution. My observer said I moved on too quickly. Next time, I’ll ask why that matters to them.
  4. They chose a private conversation to address a team concern. I suggested a follow-up date before checking what they preferred.
  5. As the season ended, I waited after asking what they wanted to carry forward. They named two options, chose one and set their own check-in.

Personal resilience story

Excerpt from Jordan’s separate story assessment.

During my junior year, I injured my ankle and missed most of the track season. I was used to being the teammate people counted on to score points. Once I couldn’t do that, I stopped going to some meets. I told myself staying home would help me recover. The harder thing to admit was that I felt embarrassed to show up when I couldn’t contribute the way I used to.

A teammate asked if I’d help a first-year runner learn our warmup. I almost said no. I went anyway. The next week, that runner led the group through the first part of the warmup on their own. I remember being proud of them and surprised by how much that mattered to me.

I still missed racing. I also started to see that paying attention and showing up for people were qualities I still had. If sharing a piece of my story would help an athlete, I’d keep it brief and return to their experience. I’d ask, “What do you miss about that role?” and give them time to answer.

Leadership presentation and Q&A

Who I am and where I’ll carry IronMind
“I’d start with a small group in the high school track program, with the school’s agreement. We’d name what changes when a role ends and what each athlete wants to carry forward.”
What I believe
“A result can matter deeply without answering who you are. Pressure can narrow what you notice. Confidence can come from following through on a choice you control. Resilience involves recovering, learning and deciding how to return. Grit includes keeping a meaningful standard and knowing when to adjust. Leadership is visible in what others count on you to do.”
How I coach
“I explain the purpose and boundaries, check my interpretation before challenging it and ask what the person wants to do and how they want to follow through.”
What if an athlete needs support beyond your role?
“I’d explain what I can help with, follow the school’s process and connect them to appropriate support. I’d explain the limits of confidentiality up front.”

Live coaching demonstration

Use the transcript for independent scoring. If you role-play the conversation, preserve the closing exchange and debrief.

Athlete “Everyone keeps telling me I should be proud, but I feel like if I stop performing at this level, I won’t know what I have left.”

Jordan “When you say you don’t know what you’ll have left, what feels most at risk?”

Athlete “Being somebody people count on. At school, I’m the runner. I don’t know what people would see if I wasn’t that.”

Jordan “Running gives you a place where you know you matter. Losing that feels bigger than losing a season. Is that close?”

Athlete “Yeah. My sister says I’m the person she calls when she needs help, but that just feels like normal stuff.”

Jordan “What does your sister count on you for?”

Athlete “I show up. I listen. I don’t make her feel stupid when she’s worried.”

Jordan “You care about being someone people can count on. Where else do those qualities show up?”

Athlete “Sometimes the younger runners ask me for help. I guess I could do more of that. Or I could talk to my sister. I don’t know which would help.”

Jordan “Start with the younger runners. Ask your coach tomorrow if you can lead part of their warmup. Text me Friday and let me know how it went.”

Athlete “Okay. I can do that, I guess.”

Evaluator “Why did you choose that approach?”

Jordan “They sounded stuck, and I wanted them to leave with something concrete. Hearing it back, they’d already named two options. I decided which one counted and gave them a deadline. I could have asked which option felt useful to them, or whether they wanted more time to think.”
